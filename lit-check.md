#### Limitations

Several limitations affect this rapid review. 
Our case ascertainment may be incomplete. 
National health authority bulletins published in non-English languages were under-indexed despite targeted native-language searches, and the web-based search tool likely returned geographically biased results. 

Ascertainment and reporting bias also operates at the case level.
Frontline and evacuated healthcare workers are more likely to receive clinical attention and confirmatory testing than other travellers. 
Our focus on confirmed cases means cases not reaching diagnostic thresholds are excluded regardless of testing sensitivity and specificity. 
We excluded suspected cases subsequently test-negative. 
This means we do not assess the risk of true Ebola infection among all travellers with suspected or non-specific symptom presentations.

Our analysis does not differentiate by purpose or type of travel, nor by the specific subpopulation at risk within outbreak source regions. 
An alternative denominator could be international travel volume over outbreak periods, rather than total reported cases per outbreak. 
This work would be complemented by more robust approaches such as mobility-based modelling to derive expected case counts. 

While our analysis evaluated across outbreaks, each outbreak saw very different levels of risk due to unique spatial and temporal flows of travel.
We found that nearly all exported cases were associated with the 2014 outbreak, affecting Sierra Leone, Guinea, Liberia, and Nigeria. 
This outbreak also saw the most extensive deployment of inbound international assistance to any Ebola outbreak, including both medical and military personnel [@loEbolaAnatomyEpidemic2017].
We consider the likelihood of case exports in the current outbreak to depend on type, scale, and changes in global mobility patterns.

#### Literature context

Previous work has evaluated role of interventions in global mobility patterns to reduce the risk of case exportations outside of Africa [@bogochAssessmentPotentialInternational2015, @otsukiReducedRiskImporting2016, @gomesAssessingInternationalSpreading2014, @cohenTravelBorderHealth2016, @copeAssessmentRiskEbola2014].
In analysis of the 2014 outbreak, implementing exit screening in affected countries was likely more efficient, feasible, and cost-effective compared to entry screening among all incoming travelers at risk [@bogochAssessmentPotentialInternational2015]. 
However, any screening is limited due to non-specificity of early symptoms [@cohenTravelBorderHealth2016].
By contrast to screening as a low-intervention approach, some work has considered restriction of movement [@gomesAssessingInternationalSpreading2014, @copeAssessmentRiskEbola2014, @loEbolaAnatomyEpidemic2017]. Even significantly restricting overall airline traffic appeared to delay rather than substantially reduce export risk (for example, an 80% airline traffic reduction permitted only a 3-4 weeks delay in risk of international case exports [@gomesAssessingInternationalSpreading2014]).
A proportionate approach with multiple options for managing travel-related risk may be most appropriate [@cohenTravelBorderHealth2016].

We emphasise the role of local, community-based case management and infection control as the most effective strategy for managing outbreak risks both overall and outside the source region [@cohenTravelBorderHealth2016, @loEbolaAnatomyEpidemic2017].

#### Some extra notes from reading about the 2014 CDC response [@cohenTravelBorderHealth2016]

During 2014-15, the CDC deployed >2,200 staff to affected countries, while developing a suite of measures to manange the risk of exported cases.

- Exit screening. CDC supported exit screening over August 2014–January 2016 in 3 countries (Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone). Approximately 300,000 travellers were screened with 0 Ebola cases detected during screening. Over the screening period, 4 asymptomatic (“not overtly symptomatic”) infected travellers were exported through air travel to other countries (United States [2 cases], United Kingdom [1], Italy [1]), and later confirmed as cases.

- Entry screening. US entry from 3 countries with Ebola outbreaks was limited to 5 airports. From October 2014 to February 2016, 38,000 travellers were screened at entry. 1,400 symptomatic travellers assessed; 0 were diagnosed; 1 asymptomatic traveller later presented with symptoms and confirmed positive. This was an aid worker in October 2014.
